<div class="abstract">

**Abstract.** Language model benchmarks measure software engineering, mathematics, and the ability to answer
graduate-level questions in subjects most users will never study. None of them measure competitive Pokémon. We
present RotomArena, a benchmark in which language models play complete Generation 9 OverUsed singles battles
against each other and against calibrated reference opponents. Ratings are fitted with a Bradley-Terry model and
reported with 95% bootstrap intervals. Across {fmtInt(meta.rated_matches)} rated matches and {fmtInt(meta.turns)}
turns, the models processed {fmtInt(meta.tokens)} tokens about Pokémon. We find that the intervals are
wide, that reasoning length is not obviously related to winning, and that a policy choosing uniformly at random
remains a meaningful baseline.

</div>

## Introduction

The field has converged on a small set of capabilities worth measuring: writing code, proving theorems, and
completing multi-step tasks in a browser. These are reasonable priorities for an industry. They are not, however,
what people ask about. People ask which model is best at Pokémon.

Competitive Pokémon is a useful testbed for several reasons. It has hidden information, simultaneous moves and
stochastic outcomes. It rewards long-horizon planning, opponent modelling, and knowledge of roughly a thousand
species, their abilities and their typical sets. It also has an active competitive community, a rigorous
open-source simulator, and a clear win condition, which is more than can be said for most agentic benchmarks.

We make three contributions. First, a harness in which any chat model can play rated games without tools, plugins
or retrieval. Second, a rating ladder that keeps evaluation cost roughly linear in the number of models. Third, a
public record of every decision every model made, including the ones it would probably prefer we did not publish.

## Related work

Games have a long history as AI benchmarks, and the rating systems used for them date back to chess
[[1]](#ref-elo). Pairwise preference ratings for language models were popularized by Chatbot Arena
[[2]](#ref-arena), which asks humans to vote between two answers; RotomArena replaces the humans with Pokémon,
which are cheaper and do not get tired. Our rating model follows Bradley and Terry [[3]](#ref-bt).

Prior work on language models in Pokémon includes PokéLLMon [[4]](#ref-pokellmon) and PokéChamp
[[5]](#ref-pokechamp), both of which build agents around a language model; PokéChamp places one inside a minimax
search and reaches expert-level play. The PokéAgent Challenge [[6]](#ref-pokeagent) turned Pokémon into a shared
competition for such agents. This benchmark is built directly on that line of work. We ask a narrower question:
what a model does with the game state and nothing else. In particular, we deliberately leave out what PokéLLMon
builds around its model: the retrieved knowledge of type matchups and move effects, the feedback after each turn,
and the safeguard against panic switching. Knowing Pokémon, and not panicking, are part of what we measure. Our reference search bot implements PokéChamp's design
without its language model, written from the paper's description, so that the search alone serves as a fixed yardstick; it should not be taken as
representative of the original, whose results are reported in [[5]](#ref-pokechamp).

## Methodology

### Format

All games are Generation 9 OverUsed singles on a local Pokémon Showdown server.
{r.team_mode === 'draft'
  ? <span>Each model plays the team it built itself, from the rules of the format alone, with Pokémon Showdown's validator as referee; a model that fails five times plays a repaired version of its last attempt (see <a href="/teams/">Teams</a>). Every language model plays every other one, once from each side of the board. Teambuilding is therefore part of what is measured, which we consider fair, since the models chose to build those teams.</span>
  : <span>Teams are fixed: each pairing of players plays a set of {l.games_per_set} games, covering both of our {r.teams.length} teams ({r.teams.map((t) => t.replace('_', ' ')).join(' and ')}) from both sides of the board. Teambuilding is therefore not measured.</span>}
Leads are not fixed: each model picks its own at team preview.

### Decisions

On every turn a model receives a plain-text description of the battle: both teams as far as they are known, the
events of the last three turns, the active Pokémon, and the list of legal actions. It replies with a short
explanation (under 80 words) and exactly one action. We do not provide type charts, damage calculations or the
opponent's likely sets. Knowing Pokémon is the skill under test.

Reasoning effort is set to {r.reasoning} wherever the provider supports it, and each answer, reasoning included,
must fit in {fmtInt(r.max_output_tokens)} output tokens. There is no chess clock; a turn is abandoned only after
{fmtInt(r.turn_timeout / 60)} minutes, which is long enough for open-weights models running on one consumer GPU.

If a model gives no usable answer (it times out, the provider fails, the answer cannot be parsed, or it names an
action that is not legal), the harness plays the first legal move on its behalf. We call this *autopilot*. Every
decision records which of these happened. A match is void if either side was on autopilot for more than
{l.max_autopilot * 100}% of its turns.

### Opponents and placement

New models are placed by a fixed schedule. They first play {l.control_games} games against Chimps at the Zoo, a control
that picks a legal action uniformly at random, named for Hans Rosling's chimpanzees.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#fn-control">1</a></sup> They then play a
set against each anchor ({anchorName}, a local language model, and our search bot), one set against a free
sparring partner, and one set against the closest-rated language model. Further sets are scheduled between models
whose rating intervals overlap the most, which is where an extra game tells us the most.

### Ratings

Ratings are Bradley-Terry strengths on the Elo scale, fitted by Newton's method with a weak prior equivalent to one
drawn game. {anchorName} is fixed at {l.anchor_rating}; every other rating is relative to it. Intervals come from
{fmtInt(l.bootstrap_samples)} parametric bootstrap refits. A rating is provisional until the model has played
{l.provisional_games} valid games.

## Limitations

The sample is small and the intervals are wide; we report them precisely so that nobody reads more into the
ordering than it supports. Two fixed teams cover a small part of the metagame. Local models are quantized and run
on a single consumer GPU, so their results describe that configuration and not the model in general. Every model
sees the same prompt, which some models will like more than others. Finally, we have not shown that skill at
Pokémon transfers to any other task, and we have not tried very hard.

## Ethics statement

All Pokémon in this study are fictional, and fainted Pokémon recover fully between games. No human annotators
were used. The electricity was real.

## Reproducibility

Every match on this site links its full battle log and every decision, with the model's explanation, its reasoning
trace when the provider returns one, token counts, latency and cost. The ratings, figures and findings on this page
are regenerated from those files by the same export that builds the site.

<h2 class="unnumbered">Appendix A. Response to Reviewer 2</h2>

**The authors evaluate a single game.** We evaluate the one that matters.

**The confidence intervals overlap.** We agree, and we have drawn them so that this is easy to see.

**Why is the anchor a heavily quantized model running on a desktop?** Because it is always available, costs
nothing per game, and cannot be deprecated by its vendor while we sleep.

**Did the authors consider that the search bot may simply be better than the language models?** Yes. See
Figure 1.

**This is not a serious benchmark.** We report bootstrap intervals for a random number generator.
